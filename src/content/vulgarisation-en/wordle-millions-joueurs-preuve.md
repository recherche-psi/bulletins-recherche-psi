Imagine that players do slightly better at a game towards the end of the day. Is that because other people have already solved it? Because the players are different? Or because the answer has begun to circulate?

A new Wordle study illustrates this difficulty.

## One word, different players

In Wordle, players identify a five-letter word in at most six guesses. For 30 days, the researchers collected statistics at three times of day.

They were interested in a hypothesis called 'morphic resonance': on this account, people who have already found the word could help subsequent players succeed without ordinary information exchange.

The snapshots contain, on average, about 17,000 players at the first time point and nearly 1.8 million at later points. They are aggregate statistics, not individual tracking of the same players.

## A measurable and very small difference

The proportion of correct first guesses rises from 0.30% to 0.48%.

To make these proportions concrete, they would correspond to **30 versus 48 correct answers per 10,000 games**. This is an illustrative conversion of percentages, not the actual observed counts.

The difference appears in the reported statistics. Its cause remains to be established.

## A big sample does not make groups comparable

With many observations, a statistical test can detect a small difference. That does not explain why it occurs.

Countries, habits and player profiles may change across the day. Answers may also circulate. In addition, cumulative snapshots reuse some of the same games, so they do not form three independent groups.

Adding observations does not automatically remove these difficulties. Distinguishing the explanations would require better control over who plays, when, and which information is available.

**The decisive question is what the comparison can distinguish. Player numbers do not replace that check.**
